A workbench has a wonderfully limited job. It holds things at a useful height
and stays still while you work. The better it does that job, the less time you
spend thinking about the bench.

{{< figure src="bench.svg" alt="An illustrative workbench drawing showing a broad top, four legs, and a low stretcher." caption="A sample notebook sketch. A study in keeping the useful parts simple." width="900" height="500" >}}

The drawing is deliberately unfinished. It leaves the interesting decisions
open: how tall, how heavy, what kind of joinery, whether there is enough room
underneath for the things that need to live there.

## Start with the work

A useful design conversation might begin with three questions:

1. What will someone actually do here?
2. What is likely to get in the way?
3. What should be easy to change later?

The answers need not produce a complicated object. They might produce a clear
surface, a little more clearance, and fewer drawers.

Software can benefit from the same exercise. Before adding another setting,
watch someone complete the ordinary task. The missing feature may turn out to
be a better default, a clearer label, or the removal of an interruption.

The bench should leave room for the work.
